## What carries over into Word

Document mode reads a page as a document rather than as a list of separate lines. That changes what you get once it's in Word:

- **Paragraphs are joined back together**, so you don't get a hard line break everywhere a line ended in the original.
- **Hyphenated words** split across a line end are put back together.
- **Headings stay headings** instead of becoming ordinary paragraphs.
- **Bulleted and numbered lists stay lists.**

The result is a document you can keep writing in, not a block of text you have to reflow by hand.

## What doesn't survive the trip

The Word file holds the text and its structure, not a copy of the page's look. These don't carry over:

- **Fonts, sizes and colors.** Restyle the text in Word afterwards.
- **Pictures, logos, charts and diagrams.** Only text is read. Labels inside a diagram may come through as stray lines that you'll want to delete.
- **The exact page layout.** Text boxes, margins and spacing aren't reproduced.

If the page needs to look exactly like the original and still be searchable, a [searchable PDF](/image-to-pdf) keeps the image and adds a hidden text layer instead.

## Download a .docx, or paste with rich copy

There are two ways into Word, and they suit different jobs.

**Download Word (.docx)** when you want a new file. It opens in Word, and Google Docs can open a .docx too, so you can upload it to your Drive and keep working there.

**Copy** when you're adding to a document you already have open. Rich copy keeps headings, lists and tables, so pasting into Word or Google Docs gives you formatted text rather than one flat block. Ctrl+Shift+C (⌘+Shift+C on a Mac) copies all the text at once.

## Prepare the page so the document comes out clean

The Word file can only be as clean as what's read from the image, and a minute spent on the image saves editing later:

- **Crop off running headers, footers and page numbers** before reading. Otherwise they're read along with the body text and end up in the middle of your paragraphs.
- **Photograph one page at a time.** Two separate photos read better than a two-page book spread, where the lines curve toward the spine.
- **Straighten pages shot at an angle** with the four-corner tool, and let Auto improve even out shadows from your hand or phone.
- **Choose the right language** if the page isn't in English, or several if it mixes languages.

For more on turning scans and phone photos into documents, see [how to convert images to editable documents](/guides/how-to-convert-images-to-editable-documents).

## Several pages into one Word file

Add every page at once and drag them into the right order. Each image becomes a page you can check against its own picture, read again or remove. The Whole document view puts the pages together, lets you search across all of them, and exports a single .docx. If you'd rather keep pages apart, download a ZIP with a separate file for each one.

## When part of the page isn't prose

Each image has its own reading mode, which you can switch without uploading it again. A page that's mostly a table reads better in Table mode, where rows and columns become an editable grid; rich copy then pastes it into Word as a real table. [Image to Excel](/image-to-excel) has more on getting tables right. Screenshots of code belong in Code mode, which keeps indentation instead of joining lines into paragraphs.
